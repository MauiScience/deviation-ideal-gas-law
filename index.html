3.6 Deviation from Ideal Gas Law

Why real gases don't perfectly obey PV = nRT — and when the difference matters most.

The ideal gas law assumes gas particles have zero volume and no attraction to each other. Real gases deviate from this model because of interparticle attractions (especially near condensation) and particle volume (especially at very high pressure).

When does a real gas behave most (and least) like an ideal gas?

Closest to ideal
Low pressure  +  High temperature

Particles are spread far apart, so the container is huge compared to the tiny space they actually occupy — particle volume barely matters. High speed means particles zip past each other before attraction has time to act, and they hit the walls hard enough that any weak attractive tug barely registers. Both real-gas effects shrink toward zero.

Farthest from ideal
High pressure  +  Low temperature

At high pressure the container shrinks until the particles' own volume becomes a real fraction of the total — the "particle volume" effect. At low temperature, particles move slowly and linger near each other, giving interparticle attraction time to actually pull them off course. Push both far enough together and the gas approaches condensation, where the ideal gas model stops making sense at all.

Compressibility factor, Z = PV / nRT

For an ideal gas, Z = 1 at every pressure — the dashed line. Real gases drift away from Z = 1. These curves are qualitative (shaped to show the correct trends), not lab-measured data.
